# Lesson 40 — PostgreSQL Replication

## Goal of This Lesson

Replication means maintaining copies of PostgreSQL data on additional database servers or subscribers.

Typical goals include:

~~~text
High availability
Read scaling
Disaster recovery support
Data distribution / integration
~~~

The most important mental model is:

~~~text
Primary PostgreSQL
      ↓
WAL changes
      ↓
Standby / Replica
~~~

Replication is closely connected to WAL, which you learned in Lesson 30.

---

# 1. Why Replication Exists

Imagine ShopHub has only one PostgreSQL server:

~~~text
Application
    ↓
PostgreSQL
~~~

If that database server fails:

~~~text
Application
    ↓
PostgreSQL ✗
    ↓
service unavailable
~~~

With a standby:

~~~text
             ┌→ Standby
Primary ─────┤
             └→ another replica
~~~

another server can maintain a copy of database changes.

Replication can improve availability, but automatic failover requires additional architecture beyond simply creating a replica.

---

# 2. Primary and Standby

In physical streaming replication, the server accepting normal writes is commonly called the **primary**.

A server receiving and replaying those changes is a **standby**.

~~~text
Writes
  ↓
Primary
  ↓ WAL stream
Standby
~~~

The standby continually applies WAL generated by the primary.

---

# 3. WAL Makes Physical Replication Possible

Recall:

~~~text
Transaction changes data
        ↓
PostgreSQL creates WAL records
        ↓
WAL describes changes needed for recovery
~~~

Streaming replication uses WAL records to reproduce changes on a standby.

~~~text
Primary
  │
  ├─ table/index changes
  │
  └─ WAL ─────────────→ Standby
                         ↓
                      replay WAL
                         ↓
                     replicated state
~~~

This is why understanding WAL before replication is important.

---

# 4. Physical Replication

Physical replication operates at PostgreSQL's physical storage/WAL level.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Primary PostgreSQL cluster
       ↓ WAL
Physical standby
~~~

It replicates the database cluster rather than selecting individual application tables in the way logical replication can.

Physical replication is commonly used for:

~~~text
standby servers
read replicas
high availability architectures
disaster recovery replicas
~~~

---

# 5. Streaming Replication

Streaming replication sends WAL records from the primary to standby servers as they are generated.

High-level flow:

~~~text
Application writes
      ↓
Primary
      ↓
WAL generated
      ↓
WAL sender
      ↓ network
WAL receiver on standby
      ↓
WAL replay
      ↓
Standby catches up
~~~

Completed WAL files do not have to wait for traditional archive-file shipping before changes can begin reaching a streaming standby.

---

# 6. Base Backup

A standby needs an initial physical copy of the PostgreSQL cluster before it can continue applying WAL.

Typical high-level flow:

~~~text
Primary
   ↓
pg_basebackup / provider mechanism
   ↓
Standby base state
   ↓
continue receiving WAL
~~~

`pg_basebackup` is commonly used in self-managed setups.

Managed providers may create replicas automatically without exposing these steps directly.

---

# 7. Replication Connection

A standby must authenticate to the primary using a role allowed to perform replication.

Conceptually:

~~~sql
CREATE ROLE replicator
WITH REPLICATION LOGIN PASSWORD '...';
~~~

`pg_hba.conf` must also permit the required replication connection in self-managed environments.

Use least privilege and secure networking/TLS where appropriate.

Do not use a PostgreSQL superuser merely because replication needs special privileges.

---

# 8. Important Primary Settings — High Level

Physical replication involves settings such as:

~~~text
wal_level
max_wal_senders
max_replication_slots
wal_keep_size
archive settings where applicable
~~~

You do not need to memorize production values.

Understand the purpose:

~~~text
Generate enough WAL information
Allow replication connections
Retain required WAL
Prevent replicas from losing required history
~~~

Managed services may configure these for you.

---

# 9. WAL Sender and WAL Receiver

On the primary:

~~~text
WAL sender process
→ sends WAL to standby
~~~

On the standby:

~~~text
WAL receiver
→ receives WAL stream
~~~

Then the standby replays the WAL.

~~~text
Primary WAL
    ↓
WAL sender
    ↓ network
WAL receiver
    ↓
Standby replay
~~~

---

# 10. Asynchronous Replication

PostgreSQL streaming replication is commonly asynchronous unless synchronous replication is configured.

Flow:

~~~text
Client
  ↓
Primary writes + commits
  ↓
Primary acknowledges client
  ↓
Standby may receive/replay shortly afterward
~~~

Benefit:

~~~text
lower write latency
~~~

Risk:

~~~text
primary fails before latest WAL reaches required standby
→ some recently committed transactions may be missing on promoted standby
~~~

This creates a potential data-loss window.

---

# 11. Synchronous Replication

With synchronous replication, commit acknowledgement can wait for required standby confirmation according to configuration.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Client
  ↓
Primary transaction
  ↓
WAL sent to synchronous standby
  ↓
required confirmation
  ↓
Primary acknowledges COMMIT
~~~

Benefit:

~~~text
stronger durability across servers
~~~

Tradeoff:

~~~text
higher commit latency
dependency on standby/network availability according to configuration
~~~

---

# 12. synchronous_commit and Synchronous Standbys

Synchronous replication behavior involves settings such as:

~~~text
synchronous_standby_names
synchronous_commit
~~~

The exact acknowledgement point can be configured.

For interview purposes:

> **Synchronous replication trades write latency/availability characteristics for stronger guarantees that committed WAL has reached required standby infrastructure.**

Do not reduce the topic to simply "sync means zero data loss" without considering configuration and failure scenarios.

---

# 13. Async vs Sync

~~~text
ASYNCHRONOUS
Primary commit
   ↓
acknowledge client
   ↓
replica catches up

Pros: lower latency
Risk: latest committed data can be lost during primary failure
~~~

~~~text
SYNCHRONOUS
Primary commit
   ↓
wait for configured standby acknowledgement
   ↓
acknowledge client

Pros: stronger cross-server durability
Tradeoff: higher latency / standby dependency
~~~

---

# 14. Replication Lag

A replica is not always exactly at the primary's current state.

Difference between primary progress and replica receive/replay progress is called **replication lag**.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Primary
Orders: 1001, 1002, 1003, 1004

Replica
Orders: 1001, 1002, 1003
                     ↑
                    lag
~~~

Replication lag can be measured in different ways, such as WAL byte positions or time-related observations.

---

# 15. Causes of Replication Lag

Common causes include:

~~~text
slow network
slow replica storage
high primary write volume
replica CPU/I/O pressure
long-running queries/conflicts on standby
WAL replay bottlenecks
temporary connectivity issues
~~~

Replication lag should be monitored, not assumed to be zero.

---

# 16. Read Replicas

A standby can often serve read-only queries when hot standby is enabled/configured.

Architecture:

~~~text
                 ┌→ Primary → writes
Application ─────┤
                 └→ Read Replica → reads
~~~

This can reduce read pressure on the primary.

Useful for:

~~~text
reporting
analytics
product browsing
non-critical dashboards
read-heavy workloads
~~~

---

# 17. Read Scaling

Suppose ShopHub has:

~~~text
10% writes
90% reads
~~~

Possible architecture:

~~~text
Writes ─────────→ Primary
                   │
                   ├─ WAL → Replica A
                   └─ WAL → Replica B

Read traffic ─────→ replicas where appropriate
~~~

But routing reads to replicas introduces consistency considerations.

---

# 18. Read-After-Write Problem

Example:

~~~text
User creates order on Primary
        ↓
COMMIT succeeds
        ↓
application immediately reads from async replica
        ↓
replica has not replayed order yet
        ↓
user sees "order missing" temporarily
~~~

This is **replica lag / read-after-write consistency** behavior.

For user-critical immediate reads, you may route the read to the primary or use an architecture that guarantees the required consistency.

---

# 19. Eventual Consistency in Read Replicas

With asynchronous replicas:

~~~text
Primary state
   ↓ shortly later
Replica state
~~~

The replica is usually eventually consistent with the primary while healthy and connected.

Therefore choose which queries may tolerate stale data.

Good candidate:

~~~text
analytics dashboard
~~~

Potentially bad candidate:

~~~text
immediately verify newly created payment/order state
~~~

Business requirements determine routing.

---

# 20. Hot Standby

Hot standby allows read-only queries while a physical standby is recovering/replaying WAL.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Standby
  ├─ replay WAL
  └─ serve read-only queries
~~~

Some long-running standby queries can conflict with WAL replay when recovery needs to change/remove row versions or structures the query depends on.

PostgreSQL has mechanisms/settings for handling such conflicts, but you should understand the tradeoff between replica query longevity and replication freshness.

---

# 21. Standby Query Conflicts

Possible situation:

~~~text
Long report query on standby
        ↓
needs old row versions
        ↓
WAL replay needs cleanup/change
        ↓
recovery conflict
~~~

PostgreSQL may delay WAL replay within configured limits or cancel conflicting standby queries.

This is one reason a replica used for analytics needs thoughtful configuration.

---

# 22. hot_standby_feedback — High Level

`hot_standby_feedback` can let a standby inform the primary about query visibility needs, reducing some recovery conflicts.

Tradeoff:

~~~text
fewer standby query cancellations
        vs
potentially more dead-row retention/bloat on primary
~~~

Do not enable/tune it without understanding the workload.

---

# 23. Replication Slots

A replication slot tells PostgreSQL that a consumer may still need certain WAL or logical changes.

Physical slot mental model:

~~~text
Primary WAL
    ↓
replication slot tracks consumer progress
    ↓
required WAL retained
~~~

This can protect a temporarily disconnected replica from losing WAL it still needs.

---

# 24. Replication Slot Danger

If a slot exists but its consumer stops advancing:

~~~text
consumer offline
      ↓
slot still requires old WAL
      ↓
PostgreSQL retains WAL
      ↓
disk usage grows
~~~

An abandoned/inactive slot can therefore become a serious storage problem.

Monitor replication slots.

---

# 25. Inspect Replication Slots

Example:

~~~sql
SELECT
  slot_name,
  slot_type,
  active
FROM pg_replication_slots;
~~~

Production monitoring should also consider retained WAL and consumer progress, not only whether the slot exists.

---

# 26. Monitor Streaming Replication

On a primary, `pg_stat_replication` provides information about connected standby replication processes.

Example:

~~~sql
SELECT
  pid,
  application_name,
  client_addr,
  state,
  sync_state
FROM pg_stat_replication;
~~~

Additional WAL position columns can help calculate receive/write/flush/replay lag.

---

# 27. WAL Positions / LSN

PostgreSQL identifies positions in WAL using **Log Sequence Numbers (LSNs)**.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Primary current WAL LSN
          ↓ compare
Standby replay LSN
          ↓
amount of WAL lag
~~~

You do not need to memorize every LSN function for a backend interview.

Understand that WAL progress can be compared precisely.

---

# 28. Failover

Failover means switching application/database service to a standby when the primary cannot serve correctly.

~~~text
Before failure:
Application → Primary
               ↓
             Standby

After failover:
Application ─────→ Promoted Standby
~~~

Promotion changes a standby into a writable primary.

---

# 29. Promotion

A standby can be promoted using PostgreSQL tooling or provider orchestration.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Standby
  ↓ promotion
New Primary
  ↓
accept writes
~~~

But promotion alone is not a complete high-availability solution.

You must also solve:

~~~text
failure detection
client routing
old-primary fencing
replica reconfiguration
data-loss policy
monitoring
~~~

These are explored more deeply in Lesson 41.

---

# 30. Split-Brain Risk

A dangerous failure mode is when two servers are incorrectly allowed to act as writable primaries.

~~~text
Client A → Primary A → writes
Client B → Primary B → different writes
~~~

Now histories diverge.

High-availability systems need reliable leader election/fencing/routing mechanisms to avoid split brain.

Replication by itself does not solve this.

---

# 31. Cascading Replication

A standby can potentially stream WAL to another standby rather than every replica connecting directly to the primary.

~~~text
Primary
   ↓
Standby A
   ↓
Standby B
~~~

This is called cascading replication.

It can reduce replication connection/network pressure on the primary in suitable architectures.

Tradeoff: downstream replicas can inherit additional lag/failure dependencies.

---

# 32. Physical Replication and Versions

Physical replication requires close PostgreSQL binary/storage compatibility because it reproduces physical database state.

Do not treat physical replication as a general mechanism for replicating between arbitrary PostgreSQL major versions.

Major-version upgrades use supported upgrade/logical migration mechanisms rather than simply attaching an incompatible physical standby.

---

# 33. Logical Replication

Logical replication operates on logical data changes rather than copying the entire physical cluster.

Core model:

~~~text
Publisher
   ↓
logical changes
   ↓
Subscriber
~~~

PostgreSQL's built-in logical replication uses a **publication/subscription** model.

---

# 34. Publication

A publication defines which table changes should be published.

Example:

~~~sql
CREATE PUBLICATION shop_publication
FOR TABLE products, orders;
~~~

Conceptually:

~~~text
products ─┐
orders ────┼→ Publication
~~~

A publication can include selected tables or broader table sets depending on configuration.

---

# 35. Subscription

On the receiving PostgreSQL database:

~~~sql
CREATE SUBSCRIPTION shop_subscription
CONNECTION 'postgresql://...'
PUBLICATION shop_publication;
~~~

High-level flow:

~~~text
Publisher tables
      ↓
Publication
      ↓
logical replication
      ↓
Subscription
      ↓
Subscriber tables
~~~

Credentials/connection strings must be handled securely in real environments.

---

# 36. What Logical Replication Replicates

Built-in logical replication focuses primarily on table data changes such as:

~~~text
INSERT
UPDATE
DELETE
TRUNCATE
~~~

depending on publication configuration/version.

Do not assume all database-level objects automatically replicate.

For example, schema definitions and many DDL changes require separate migration/schema management.

---

# 37. Schema Management with Logical Replication

Suppose you add a column on the publisher.

Do not assume built-in logical replication will automatically perform the same DDL on the subscriber.

Conceptually:

~~~text
Application migration
      ↓
Publisher schema change

Separate coordinated migration
      ↓
Subscriber schema compatibility
~~~

Logical replication requires schema compatibility planning.

---

# 38. Replica Identity

For logical replication of UPDATE/DELETE, PostgreSQL needs a way to identify the affected row on the subscriber.

A primary key is typically ideal.

PostgreSQL uses **replica identity** to determine which old-row information is available for identifying rows.

High-level rule:

> Tables participating in logical UPDATE/DELETE replication should have an appropriate key/replica identity strategy.

---

# 39. Physical vs Logical Replication

~~~text
PHYSICAL REPLICATION
Level: storage/WAL physical changes
Scope: whole cluster/standby architecture
Typical use: HA, read replicas, DR
Schema: physically reproduced
Version flexibility: tightly constrained
~~~

~~~text
LOGICAL REPLICATION
Level: logical table changes
Scope: selected tables/publications
Typical use: data distribution, migrations, integrations
Schema: must be coordinated
Version flexibility: greater for supported combinations
~~~

Choose based on the problem being solved.

---

# 40. Logical Replication Use Cases

Examples:

~~~text
replicate selected tables
migrate data between PostgreSQL environments
zero/low-downtime upgrade strategies
feed a reporting database
consolidate/distribute selected datasets
~~~

It is not simply a replacement for physical HA replication.

---

# 41. Logical Replication Conflicts

If applications write conflicting data independently on both publisher and subscriber, replication can encounter constraint/data conflicts.

Built-in logical replication is not automatically a conflict-free multi-primary system.

Design write ownership clearly.

---

# 42. Replication Is Not Backup

This is an important interview question.

Suppose:

~~~sql
DELETE FROM orders;
~~~

on the primary.

Replication can propagate that deletion:

~~~text
Primary
DELETE orders
   ↓
replication
   ↓
Replica
DELETE orders
~~~

Now both copies have the mistake.

Backup/PITR exists to recover historical state.

---

# 43. Replication Is Not Automatic HA

Having:

~~~text
Primary + Replica
~~~

does not automatically mean:

~~~text
zero downtime
automatic failover
correct client routing
no data loss
split-brain protection
~~~

High availability requires orchestration and operational design.

Lesson 41 covers this next.

---

# 44. Replication and Backups Together

Strong production architecture often uses both:

~~~text
Primary
   ├─→ Standby / replica
   │      → availability/read scaling
   │
   └─→ backups + WAL archive
          → historical recovery / PITR
~~~

These mechanisms solve different failure modes.

---

# 45. ShopHub Example

Imagine ShopHub grows significantly.

Architecture:

~~~text
                    ┌→ Read Replica A → product/catalog reads
Users → Application ─┤
                    └→ Primary → checkout/order/inventory writes
                           │
                           └→ Read Replica B → reporting
~~~

For checkout:

~~~text
inventory decrement
payment/order state
immediate order confirmation
~~~

you may prefer primary reads/writes where fresh consistency is required.

For analytics:

~~~text
daily sales dashboard
product browsing analytics
historical reports
~~~

a slightly lagging replica may be acceptable.

---

# 46. CareerLoop Example

For CareerLoop at small scale:

~~~text
Application
   ↓
Managed PostgreSQL primary
~~~

may be completely sufficient.

Do not add replicas merely because production systems can have them.

When requirements become:

~~~text
higher availability
read-heavy analytics
regional recovery
provider SLA requirements
~~~

managed replicas/HA can be introduced.

Architecture should follow requirements, not resume keywords.

---

# 47. Monitoring Replication

Monitor at least:

~~~text
replica connection state
WAL send/receive/replay positions
replication lag
replication slot retention
standby disk usage
standby query conflicts
network health
replica replay health
~~~

Alerts should trigger before lag/storage becomes an outage.

---

# 48. Replication and Network Failure

If network connectivity between primary and replica fails:

~~~text
Primary continues generating WAL
      ↓
Replica cannot receive it temporarily
      ↓
lag increases
~~~

Recovery depends on whether required WAL remains available through:

~~~text
WAL retention
replication slots
WAL archive
~~~

If required WAL is gone, the standby may need to be rebuilt from a new base backup.

---

# 49. Replication Slot vs wal_keep_size — Mental Model

`wal_keep_size` helps retain a minimum amount of past WAL in `pg_wal` for standby needs.

A replication slot instead tracks a consumer's required WAL position and can force PostgreSQL to retain required WAL until that consumer advances, subject to relevant safeguards/settings.

~~~text
wal_keep_size
→ size-oriented minimum retention

replication slot
→ consumer-progress-oriented retention
~~~

Slots provide stronger consumer tracking but can cause disk growth if neglected.

---

# 50. Security

Replication traffic and credentials are sensitive.

Use:

~~~text
dedicated replication role
least privilege
restricted pg_hba.conf rules
private networking where possible
TLS where required
secret management
monitoring
~~~

Do not expose replication access broadly to the internet.

# Common Mistakes

## 51. Assuming Replica Data Is Always Current

Asynchronous replication can lag.

## 52. Sending Every Read to a Replica

Some workflows require immediate read-after-write consistency.

## 53. Treating Replication as Backup

Bad data changes can replicate too.

## 54. Treating a Replica as Automatic High Availability

Failover detection, promotion, routing, fencing, and recovery still need design.

## 55. Ignoring Replication Slots

Inactive slots can retain large amounts of WAL and fill storage.

## 56. Using Long Queries on a Standby Without Understanding Recovery Conflicts

Hot-standby reads can conflict with WAL replay.

## 57. Assuming Logical Replication Copies All DDL

Schema changes require coordination.

## 58. Assuming Logical Replication Is Multi-Master by Default

Conflicting writes require deliberate architecture.

## 59. Choosing Synchronous Replication Without Considering Latency

Cross-server acknowledgement can increase commit latency and affect availability characteristics.

## 60. Adding Replicas Before They Are Needed

Replication adds operational complexity. Use it to satisfy real availability/scaling/recovery requirements.

# Interview Revision

## What is PostgreSQL replication?

The process of maintaining copies of PostgreSQL data/changes on other servers or subscribers for availability, read scaling, disaster recovery support, migration, or data distribution.

## How does physical streaming replication work?

The primary generates WAL, WAL sender processes stream it to standbys, and standbys receive and replay the WAL to reproduce the primary's physical changes.

## What is asynchronous replication?

The primary can acknowledge a commit before the standby has confirmed the required WAL, giving lower latency but allowing a potential recent-data-loss window during failure.

## What is synchronous replication?

The primary waits for configured standby acknowledgement before reporting commit according to the configured synchronous durability policy, improving cross-server durability at the cost of latency/availability tradeoffs.

## What is replication lag?

The difference between the primary's WAL/data progress and how far the replica has received, flushed, or replayed changes.

## What is a read replica?

A standby used to serve read-only queries, reducing read workload on the primary.

## What is the read-after-write problem?

An async replica may not yet contain a change that just committed on the primary, so an immediate replica read can return stale data.

## What is a replication slot?

A server-side object that tracks a replication consumer's progress and retains required WAL/logical changes until the consumer advances, subject to configuration.

## Why can replication slots be dangerous?

An inactive slot can prevent WAL cleanup and cause storage usage to grow.

## Physical vs logical replication?

Physical replication reproduces the PostgreSQL cluster using WAL/storage-level changes and is common for HA/read replicas. Logical replication publishes logical table changes and supports selected-table replication and migration/integration use cases.

## What are publications and subscriptions?

A publication defines logical changes to publish; a subscription connects to publications and applies those changes on the subscriber.

## Is replication a backup?

No. Replication can copy accidental deletes or corruption-related logical changes. Backups/PITR preserve historical recovery options.

## Is replication automatically high availability?

No. HA also requires failure detection, promotion, routing, fencing, monitoring, and operational recovery design.

## Key Takeaway

> **PostgreSQL replication uses WAL or logical change streams to maintain additional copies of data. Physical streaming replication is the foundation for common standby/read-replica and HA architectures, while logical replication is useful for selected-table distribution and migrations. Always account for replication lag, read-after-write consistency, replication-slot retention, and synchronous/asynchronous durability tradeoffs—and remember that replication is neither a backup nor a complete high-availability solution by itself.**
